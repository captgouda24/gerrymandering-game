The '''2026 United States state legislative elections''' were held on November 3, 2026, for 88 state legislative chambers in 46 states.<ref name="seatsup"/> Across the [[U.S. state|fifty states]], approximately 60% of [[upper house]] seats and 92% of [[lower house]] seats will be up for election. Additionally, six territorial chambers will be up in four territories and the [[Washington, D.C.|District of Columbia]]. These [[United States midterm election|elections]] will take place concurrently with other [[2026 United States elections|state and local elections]], including [[2026 United States gubernatorial elections|gubernatorial elections in multiple states]].

== Background ==
[[File:United States state legislatures 2025.svg|thumb|upright=1.2|Partisan control of state legislatures following the [[2024 United States state legislative elections|2024]] and [[2025 United States state legislative elections|2025 elections]]{{legend|#f33|Republican control (27)}}{{legend|#33f|Democratic control (18)}}{{legend|#829|Split control (4)}}{{legend|#000|Nonpartisan legislature (1)}}]]
The 2026 state legislative elections were held as part of the [[United States midterm election|midterm elections]] during the [[Second presidency of Donald Trump|second presidency]] of [[Donald Trump]]. Republicans had seen very modest [[Coattail effect|coattails]] in the [[2024 United States state legislative elections|2024 legislative elections]], flipping a net of just over 50 seats from the Democrats and breaking Democratic [[Government trifecta|trifectas]] in two states, but establishing no new trifectas of their own.<ref name="CO">{{Cite web |last1=Barajas |first1=Michael |last2=Nichanian |first2=Daniel |date=2024-12-04 |title=The GOP Came Out Ahead in Legislative Races, But Their Gains Were Modest and Uneven |url=https://boltsmag.org/legislative-elections-2024/ |access-date=2025-01-31 |website=Bolts |language=en}}</ref> Democrats performed very well in the [[2025 United States state legislative elections|2025 elections]], making substantial gains in [[2025 Virginia House of Delegates election|Virginia]] and [[2025 New Jersey General Assembly election|New Jersey]] and performing very well in [[2025 United States state legislative elections#Special elections|special elections]].<ref name=":4">{{Cite web |last=Nichanian |first=Daniel |date=2025-12-10 |title=In 2025, Democrats Flipped 21 Percent of GOP-Held Legislative Seats |url=https://boltsmag.org/legislative-elections-results-2025/ |access-date=2025-12-11 |website=Bolts |language=en}}</ref> Entering 2026, Republicans fully control 23 state governments, Democrats control 16, with 11 states under split control.

Republicans have controlled a sizeable majority of state legislative chambers across the country since the [[2010 United States state legislative elections|2010 elections]], giving them substantial clout over a number of policy issues, particularly redistricting prior to the [[2010 United States redistricting cycle|2010 redistricting cycle]]. Democrats have slowly made gains in chamber control since [[First presidency of Donald Trump|Donald Trump's first term]] began, particularly in [[2018 United States state legislative elections|2018]] and [[2022 United States state legislative elections|2022]], but the Republican advantage has persisted.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Fertig |first=Natalie |date=2026-04-04 |title=Republicans are losing clout in statehouses in another possible sign of midterm trouble |url=https://www.politico.com/news/2026/04/04/republicans-state-midterms-losses-00856496 |access-date=2026-04-04 |website=POLITICO |language=en}}</ref>

== Issues ==
State legislative elections have taken on increased prominence in the eyes of national political parties due to the ongoing [[2025–2026 United States redistricting|mid-decade congressional redistricting]] frenzy taking place across the country, especially in the wake of the ''[[Louisiana v. Callais]]'' decision, which allowed several Republican-led states in the [[Southern United States|South]] to eliminate [[List of majority-minority United States congressional districts#African-American|majority-Black congressional districts]]. With Republicans gaining a clear advantage for the [[2026 United States House of Representatives elections|2026 elections]], Democrats have turned their focus to state legislatures in key states with hopes of regaining the redistricting advantage for the 2028 elections.<ref name=":1">{{Cite web |last=Edelman |first=Adam |date=2026-05-13 |title=Democrats turn to state legislative races to catch up in the redistricting battle |url=https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/democrats-turn-state-legislative-races-catch-redistricting-battle-rcna344719 |access-date=2026-05-14 |website=NBC News |language=en}}</ref> Affordability and the proliferation of [[AI data center|AI data centers]] have also emerged as key issues in several states.<ref name=":2" />

== Campaign ==
To build momentum for the [[2026 United States elections|broader national elections]], Democrats have recruited substantially more candidates to run for state legislative offices than in previous cycles. This includes contesting every seat in Texas, South Carolina, and Maryland, and breaking decades-old recruitment highs in states such as Georgia, Indiana, and North Carolina.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Murphy |first=Tim |title=Downballot Democrats are smashing recruitment records |url=https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2026/06/downballot-democratic-candidate-recruitment-2026/ |access-date=2026-06-23 |website=Mother Jones |language=en-US}}</ref> This has led to a drop in the number of uncontested legislative seats compared to previous years. Only 33% of state legislative seats have one candidate running in the general election, the lowest since [[2018 United States state legislative elections|2018]], and only 20% of incumbents are running unopposed, far lower than any election cycle since [[2010 United States state legislative elections|2010]].<ref>{{Cite web |last=Kramer |first=Bill |date=October 16, 2026 |title=2026 Primary Elections: Fewer Uncontested State Legislative Races |url=https://www.multistate.us/insider/2026/10/16/uncontested-state-legislative-races-drop-to-lowest-level-since-2018 |access-date=2026-10-17 |website=MultiState |language=en-US}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=Maxwell |first=Nathan |date=October 15, 2026 |title=Record low number of uncontested incumbents in 2026 state legislative general elections |url=https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/10/15/record-low-number-of-uncontested-incumbents-in-2026-state-legislative-general-elections/ |access-date=October 16, 2026 |website=[[Ballotpedia]]}}</ref>

Democrats are heavily targeting control of the [[Arizona State Legislature|Arizona]], [[Michigan Legislature|Michigan]], [[Minnesota Legislature|Minnesota]], [[New Hampshire General Court|New Hampshire]], [[Pennsylvania General Assembly|Pennsylvania]], and [[Wisconsin legislatures]], while seeking to gain [[supermajorities]] in [[Oregon Legislative Assembly|Oregon]] and [[Washington State Legislature|Washington]] while breaking Republican supermajorites in the [[Iowa House of Representatives|Iowa House]], [[Nebraska Legislature]], and [[North Carolina Senate]].<ref name=":1" /><ref name=":2">{{Cite web |last=Hardy |first=Kevin |date=2026-07-16 |title=Democrats have a shot at flipping state legislatures across the country • Stateline |url=https://stateline.org/2026/07/16/democrats-have-a-shot-at-flipping-state-legislatures-across-the-country/ |access-date=2026-07-17 |website=Stateline |language=en-US}}</ref> National Democratic groups have raised over $100 million to support legislative Democrats across the country, matching what national Republicans raised for the [[2024 United States state legislative elections|2024 elections]].<ref>{{Cite news |last=Merica |first=Dan |date=2026-10-15 |title=Democratic group substantially increases fundraising to win in state legislatures |url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/10/15/democratic-group-substantially-increases-fundraising-seize-state-legislatures/ |access-date=2026-10-16 |work=The Washington Post |language=en-US |issn=0190-8286}}</ref>

Several states had highly contentious [[Primary election|primary elections]] which shifted power in chambers already safely held by one party. In the [[Indiana Senate]], where a majority of the chamber's Republicans rebuked [[Donald Trump]]'s [[2025 Indiana redistricting attempt|mid-decade redistricting push]] as a part of his [[2025–2026 United States redistricting|broader attempts]] to maintain Republican control of congress after the [[2026 United States House of Representatives elections|midterms]], six of the defiant members [[2026 Indiana Senate election#Primary election|lost renomination]], while another two retired.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Bradner |first=Eric |date=2026-05-06 |title=Trump gets revenge, and other takeaways from Tuesday’s Indiana and Ohio primaries |url=https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/05/politics/takeaways-indiana-ohio-primary-elections |access-date=2026-06-26 |website=CNN |language=en}}</ref> In New York, six{{Efn|One race has not yet been called}} new candidates endorsed by the [[Democratic Socialists of America]] won primaries across both chambers, nearly doubling the size of the party's caucus.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Bragg |first=Chris |last2=Garber |first2=Nick |date=June 24, 2026 |title=NY Primary Results: DSA Set to Pick Up 6+ Seats in Albany |url=https://nysfocus.com/2026/06/24/ny-primary-election-results-dsa-state-legislature-2026 |access-date=2026-06-26 |website=New York Focus |language=en}}</ref> The DSA made gains across the country, winning over 60 Democratic primaries, although their direct influence is expected to be low, representing fewer than 1% of legislative seats nationwide.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Kamarck |first=Elaine |last2=Mahtani |first2=Riana |last3=Kraemer |first3=Gabriel |date=August 19, 2026 |title=Democratic socialist candidates show gains but limited reach in 2026 |url=https://www.brookings.edu/articles/democratic-socialist-candidates-show-gains-but-limited-reach-in-2026/ |access-date=2026-10-17 |website=Brookings |language=en-US}}</ref> Two legislative leaders have also been defeated. [[Utah Senate]] President [[Stuart Adams]] lost in a race dominated by a contentious planned [[AI data center]], while [[North Carolina Senate]] leader [[Phil Berger (politician)|Phil Berger]] narrowly lost his primary as well.<ref>{{Cite web |last=McKellar |first=Katie |date=2026-06-24 |title=Stuart Adams is out: Utah Senate president concedes to Republican challenger |url=https://utahnewsdispatch.com/2026/06/23/utah-senate-president-stuart-adams-republican-primary-election-results/ |access-date=2026-06-29 |website=Utah News Dispatch |language=en-US}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=Guilfoil |first=Kyla |date=2026-03-24 |title=N.C. Senate's top Republican concedes in primary decided by 23 votes |url=https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/elections/nc-senate-republican-leader-phil-berger-concedes-recount-sam-page-rcna265000 |access-date=2026-06-29 |website=NBC News |language=en}}</ref>

===States===
Regularly scheduled elections were held in 88 of the 99 state legislative chambers in the United States. Nationwide, regularly scheduled elections were held for 6,064 of the 7,383 legislative seats. Most legislative chambers will hold elections for all seats, but some legislative chambers that use staggered elections hold elections for only a portion of the total seats in the chamber. The chambers that are not up for election either hold regularly scheduled elections in odd-numbered years, or have four-year terms and hold all regularly scheduled elections in presidential election years.

Note that this table only covers regularly scheduled elections; additional special elections will likely take place concurrently with these regularly scheduled elections.

== Electoral predictions ==
Several sites and individuals publish predictions of competitive chambers. These predictions look at factors such as the strength of the party, the strength of the candidates, and the partisan leanings of the state (reflected in part by the state's [[Cook Partisan Voting Index]] rating). The predictions assign ratings to each chambers, with the rating indicating the predicted advantage that a party has in winning that election.

Early predictions saw a slightly more competitive state legislative landscape than at this point in previous election cycles, with most of the competitive being Republican-held. Democrats hope to capitalize on a potential [[Wave elections in the United States|wave election]] to win control of these chambers and break Republican [[supermajorities]] in others.<ref name=":0">{{Cite web |last=Jacobson |first=Louis |date=2026-01-22 |title=Handicapping The 2026 State Legislative Map: A First Look |url=https://centerforpolitics.org/crystalball/handicapping-the-2026-state-legislative-map-a-first-look/ |access-date=2026-01-22 |website=Sabato's Crystal Ball |language=en-US}}</ref> By the fall, a number of chambers shifted towards the Democrats as their national standings and polling improved, particularly in the [[Midwestern United States|Midwest]], where Republicans control a number of competitive chambers in [[Swing state|swing states]]. [[Sabato's Crystal Ball]] forecasts mild Democratic gains, while [[State Navigate]] is much more bullish, anticipating Democrats flipping over 400 legislative seats across the country.<ref name="Website-1">{{Cite web |last=Jacobson |first=Louis |date=October 16, 2026 |title=The Key State Legislative Races: GOP Plays Defense in Midwest and Elsewhere |url=https://centerforpolitics.org/crystalball/the-key-state-legislative-races-gop-plays-defense-in-midwest-and-elsewhere/ |access-date=October 16, 2026 |website=[[Sabato's Crystal Ball]]}}</ref><ref name="Website-2" />

Ratings are designated as follows:
* "<u>Tossup</u>": No advantage
* "<u>Tilt</u>": Advantage that is not quite as strong as "lean"
* "<u>Lean</u>": Slight advantage
* "<u>Likely</u>": Significant, but surmountable, advantage{{efn|Sabato's Crystal Ball additionally uses the "Likely" characterization to indicate chambers where a shift in control is unlikely, but the minority party could make significant gains and/or break a legislative [[supermajority#State government|supermajority]].}}
* "<u>Safe</u>": Near-certain chance of victory

== State summaries ==

=== Alabama ===
{{main|2026 Alabama Senate election|2026 Alabama House of Representatives election}}

All of the seats of the [[Alabama Legislature]] are up for election. Both chambers have been controlled by the Republican Party since 2011. The Senate election originally would have taken place under slightly different maps from the [[2022 Alabama Senate election|2022 election]] after a federal judge overturned two [[Montgomery, Alabama|Montgomery]]-area districts over [[Gerrymandering in the United States|vote dilution]] concerns, creating an additional majority-[[African Americans|Black]] district there.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Barrett |first=Anna |date=2025-11-18 |title=Federal judge approves new Alabama Senate map redrawing Montgomery districts |url=https://alabamareflector.com/2025/11/18/federal-judge-approves-new-alabama-senate-map-redrawing-montgomery-districts/ |access-date=2026-01-21 |website=[[Alabama Reflector]] |language=en-US}}</ref> This decision was stayed after the ''[[Louisiana v. Callais]]'' [[Supreme Court of the United States|Supreme Court]] decision, which significantly weakened the part of the [[Voting Rights Act of 1965|Voting Rights Act]] that was used to justify the original decision, returning the elections to the original maps.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Barrett |first=Anna |date=2026-05-29 |title=Federal appeals court allows Alabama to use 2021 state Senate map |url=https://alabamareflector.com/2026/05/29/federal-appeals-court-allows-alabama-to-use-2021-state-senate-map/ |access-date=2026-08-03 |website=Alabama Reflector |language=en-US}}</ref>

There are currently 88 state legislative special elections scheduled for 2026.<ref>{{cite web |title=State legislative special elections, 2026|website=[[Ballotpedia]]|url=https://ballotpedia.org/State_legislative_special_elections,_2026|access-date=October 12, 2025}}</ref> More than half of all states have procedures for special state legislative elections.<ref>{{cite web |title=How are vacancies filled in state legislatures? |url=https://ballotpedia.org/How_vacancies_are_filled_in_state_legislatures |website=[[Ballotpedia]] |access-date=18 December 2024}}</ref> Democrats have performed especially well in special elections, outperforming [[Kamala Harris]]' [[2024 United States presidential election|2024 performance]] by an average of 10 [[Percentage point|percentage points]] and flipping six seats across the country.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Special Election Swing Analysis |url=https://www.multistate.us/elections/special-swing |access-date=2026-05-06 |website=www.multistate.us}}</ref> Many of the seats Democrats have flipped came from districts which voted strongly for [[Donald Trump]] in 2024, and the party's consistent overperformances indicate a wide enthusiasm gap between Democratic and Republican voters. They also, however, signal a shift in the electorate's educational gap, with highly educated voters, who are more likely to vote in special elections, voting for Democrats more often than in previous decades.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Somasundaram |first=Praveena |date=2026-08-19 |title=What it means that Democrats are winning special elections in Trump country |url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/08/19/what-it-means-that-democrats-are-winning-special-elections-trump-country/ |access-date=2026-08-19 |work=The Washington Post |language=en-US |issn=0190-8286}}</ref> 

Several of these special elections have decided control of their respective legislative chambers, although none have resulted in a shift in power. Democrats maintained control of the [[Michigan Senate]], [[Pennsylvania House of Representatives]], and [[Virginia Senate]] while maintaining the even split in the [[Minnesota House of Representatives]].
